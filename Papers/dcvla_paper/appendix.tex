\section{Appendix}


\subsection{Experimental Environment}
All experiments are conducted under a unified hardware and software configuration to ensure fair comparison across methods and benchmarks.
Unless otherwise stated, the evaluation environment is as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GPU:} NVIDIA RTX 5880 Ada GPU with 48\,GB memory.
    \item \textbf{CPU:} Dual-socket AMD EPYC 9654 processors (96 cores per socket, 192 cores total, 384 threads).
    \item \textbf{CUDA / Driver:} CUDA~13.0 with NVIDIA driver version~580.65.06.
\end{itemize}

All latency measurements are obtained using CUDA events and exclude environment simulation and robot actuation overhead.

\subsection{Vision Encoder Configuration}
Unless otherwise specified, the visual observation at each timestep is resized to $224\times224$ and processed by a ViT-based vision encoder.
Images are partitioned into non-overlapping patches of size $14\times14$, resulting in
\[
P = (224 / 14)^2 = 256
\]
visual patches per frame.
We evaluate two widely used vision backbones in OpenVLA-style models: DINOv2 and SigLIP.
These configurations are kept identical across baselines and CTR-enabled variants to isolate the effect of coordinated token reuse.

\subsection{CTR Configuration and Latency Accounting}
Unless otherwise specified, CTR uses grayscale patch difference for temporal consistency, a difference threshold of $0.004$, top-$K$ task-relevance selection with $K=160$, and a keyframe interval of $5$ control steps.
Patch-level attention scores are computed from the previous-step text-to-vision attention: attention maps are averaged over heads, then the text-query rows are averaged for each vision-token column, and the top-$K$ visual patches are marked task-relevant and forced to recompute.
The LLM-side layer schedule follows the entropy-based cache-reuse schedule used by the VLA-Cache component.

OpenVLA-style models use two vision-encoder branches, DINOv2 and SigLIP.
Accordingly, main-table model-core latency and TFLOPs are computed as
\[
    \mathrm{Total} = \mathrm{VE}_{\mathrm{DINOv2}} + \mathrm{VE}_{\mathrm{SigLIP}} + \mathrm{LLM}.
\]
When a component ablation reports a single VE latency or TFLOPs value, it is reported per vision branch; this is why the main-table totals correspond to $2\times$VE + LLM in the OpenVLA ablation.
All reported efficiency numbers exclude simulator stepping, rendering, and robot actuation.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
CTR is implemented as an inference-time cache mechanism and does not change the checkpoint weights, tokenizer, image processor, or action head.
At timestep $t=0$ and at every keyframe, all visual patches are recomputed and both VE and LLM caches are refreshed.
For non-keyframe steps, the same patch-level mask is applied to both branches of the vision encoder and to the corresponding visual-token positions in the LLM prefill.
Text tokens are always recomputed; reuse decisions are applied only to visual tokens.

The text-to-vision attention signal is extracted from the previous dense or CTR forward pass.
We aggregate attention by averaging over heads and over text-query positions, yielding one scalar relevance score per visual patch.
The top-$K$ visual patches under this relevance score are treated as task-relevant and are excluded from reuse even when their pixel difference is small.
This previous-step attention is deliberately used as a conservative signal: it avoids requiring an additional current-frame LLM pass solely for mask construction.

For latency measurement, CUDA events are placed around the model-core components.
The reported VE latency is the average latency of one vision branch, while the main OpenVLA total uses both DINOv2 and SigLIP branches.
The reported LLM latency includes multimodal prefill and action-token decoding inside the language model, but excludes simulator stepping, rendering, image acquisition, and robot actuation.
The TFLOPs estimates count arithmetic operations in the dense or reuse-enabled model-core path and do not count Python dispatch or cache bookkeeping as FLOPs; these engineering costs are instead reflected in measured CUDA latency.

\subsection{Benchmarks}
We evaluate CTR on two complementary simulation benchmarks that cover a wide spectrum of manipulation behaviors.

\textbf{LIBERO} is used as the primary benchmark and includes four task suites:
\textit{Spatial} (precise object placement and spatial reasoning),
\textit{Object} (single-object manipulation with diverse geometries),
\textit{Goal} (goal-conditioned, multi-step manipulation),
and \textit{Long} (long-horizon tasks requiring temporal consistency).
For LIBERO experiments, we use the official OpenVLA-7B checkpoint that has been fine-tuned on the corresponding LIBERO task suites.

To assess generalization beyond LIBERO, we additionally evaluate on \textbf{SimplerEnv}, a tabletop manipulation benchmark with different object layouts and visual statistics.
We report results on three representative tasks: \textit{Move Near Object}, \textit{Pick Coke Can}, and \textit{Drawer Operations}.
For SimplerEnv, we use the original OpenVLA-7B checkpoint without any task-specific fine-tuning.
All CTR hyperparameters are kept identical to those used in LIBERO experiments.

\subsection{Model Variants and Checkpoints}
We evaluate two VLA backbones: OpenVLA and OpenVLA-OFT.
All experiments use officially released OpenVLA-7B checkpoints.
CTR is applied strictly at inference time and does not modify model weights, training data, or optimization procedures.
Both the baseline and CTR-enabled models share identical architectures and token layouts.

\subsection{Detailed Dataflow of Vision-Encoder Reuse}
CTR preserves the original visual token layout while reducing the amount of current-frame computation inside the vision encoder.
Let $P$ denote the full number of visual patches and let $D_t$ denote the patches that are either temporally dynamic or task-relevant at timestep $t$.
For each transformer block, only tokens in $D_t$ issue current-frame queries and pass through the block MLP; the remaining tokens reuse cached sublayer states from the previous timestep.
The dynamic key/value states are written back to the per-block cache, and the block output is scattered back to the original $P$-token order.
Thus, the downstream projector and LLM receive the same full-length visual sequence as in the dense model.

This implementation differs from physically shortening the visual grid.
Dense ViT attention uses a $P\times P$ query--key interaction.
CTR changes the dominant attention interaction to $D_t\times P$: dynamic/task-relevant queries attend to the full cached context, while reused tokens do not issue queries.
This design keeps the representation shape compatible with the original OpenVLA pipeline and avoids retraining or changing the projector/LLM input contract.

\subsection{Expanded Complexity Accounting}
The main text gives the compact complexity equations.
Here we spell out which computation each term corresponds to.
For a ViT block with hidden size $d_v$ and MLP width $m_v$, dense inference over $P$ visual patches has three dominant components:
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{VE}}^{\mathrm{dense}}
    \approx 4P d_v^2 + 2P^2 d_v + 2P d_v m_v.
\end{equation}
The first term approximates Q/K/V and output projections, the second term approximates attention score and value aggregation, and the third term approximates the two-layer MLP.
If CTR recomputes only $D_t$ patches, the corresponding current-frame compute becomes
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{VE}}^{\mathrm{CTR}}
    \approx 4D_t d_v^2 + 2D_tP d_v + 2D_t d_v m_v + C_{\mathrm{cache}},
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{cache}}$ denotes cache read/write and scatter overhead.
The attention term is $D_tP$, not $D_t^2$, because recomputed query tokens still attend to the full cached visual context.
This is the intended tradeoff: CTR keeps the original full-token interface while reducing query-side attention, projection, and MLP computation.

For the LLM, let $L_x$ be the number of text/action-prompt tokens, $P$ the number of visual tokens, and $D_t$ the number of visual tokens that remain in the recompute set at reuse-enabled decoder layers.
The dense multimodal prefill length is $L=L_x+P$, while the recomputed length under reuse is $L'=L_x+D_t$.
For a decoder block with hidden size $d_l$ and MLP width $m_l$, the idealized dense and reuse-enabled prefill costs are
\begin{align}
    C_{\mathrm{LLM}}^{\mathrm{dense}} &\approx 4L d_l^2 + 2L^2 d_l + 2L d_l m_l, \\
    C_{\mathrm{LLM}}^{\mathrm{CTR}} &\approx 4L' d_l^2 + 2(L')^2 d_l + 2L' d_l m_l + C_{\mathrm{merge}}.
\end{align}
$C_{\mathrm{merge}}$ captures cache merging and indexing overhead and is not counted as arithmetic FLOPs.
This distinction is why TFLOPs reduction and wall-clock latency reduction need not be identical.
The complexity analysis measures the arithmetic avoided by not recomputing selected visual-token K/V states, while the latency tables report the actual CUDA time including cache-management overhead.

\subsection{Causal Dependency of LLM-Side Cache Reuse}
A natural concern is whether LLM-side KV reuse is compatible with causal attention.
If one requires exact equivalence to a dense causal transformer for an arbitrary new input, then non-prefix KV reuse is not algebraically exact: the hidden state of a later token can depend on earlier tokens, so changing an early token may affect all later K/V representations.
CTR therefore does not claim exact dense equivalence for arbitrary token changes.
Instead, it uses KV reuse as a controlled temporal approximation for closed-loop VLA inference.

The approximation is constrained in three ways.
First, the reused positions are selected from patches that are visually stable between consecutive frames.
Second, visual patches that receive high previous-step text-to-vision attention are always recomputed, even if their pixels appear stable.
Third, periodic keyframes force a full refresh, limiting long-term error accumulation.
In other words, CTR reuses cached K/V only for positions that are both temporally stable and low in task relevance, while dynamic or instruction-critical patches are recomputed.

This also explains why the same mask is useful across the VE and LLM.
The VE-side reuse reduces current-frame visual computation while keeping the full visual sequence layout.
The LLM-side reuse then applies the same patch decision to the multimodal prefill cache, so the language model does not recompute K/V for visual tokens that the vision encoder has already treated as stable.

\paragraph{Error intuition.}
For an attention output $o_i=\sum_j \alpha_{ij} V_j$, reusing the cached value at position $j$ contributes an error proportional to both its attention mass $\alpha_{ij}$ and its cross-frame value drift.
The CTR mask is designed to reduce both factors: temporal filtering selects patches with small representation drift, and text-to-vision attention filtering removes high-mass task-relevant patches from the reuse set.
This does not make reuse exact, but it makes the approximation aligned with the error terms that matter most for the next action prediction.

\subsection{Lightweight Probe of LLM Cache-Reuse Assumptions}
To check whether the mask behaves as intended, we run a lightweight diagnostic on the OpenVLA LIBERO-Spatial checkpoint.
The probe uses ten synthetic consecutive frame pairs and performs dense forward passes for both frames.
For each pair, we build the CTR reuse mask using the same grayscale-difference threshold ($0.004$), static top-$K$ ($160$), and attention top-$K$ ($160$) as in the main configuration.
We then compare dense cross-frame K/V drift and previous-step text-to-vision attention mass for tokens selected for reuse versus recomputation.
K/V drift is measured as the average RMS change of dense decoder K/V states across layers and heads.
We also report a simple attention-weighted drift proxy, computed as $\mathrm{KV\ drift}\times\mathrm{Attn.\ mean}$, to reflect that the effect of a reused value on the attention output is modulated by its attention mass.
This probe is not intended as a new benchmark; it only tests whether the mask selects tokens that are less sensitive under dense computation.

The reuse-selected tokens have $67.8\%$ of the K/V drift of the recompute-selected tokens and only $12.0\%$ of their mean text-to-vision attention mass.
Therefore, their attention-weighted drift proxy is only about $8.1\%$ of that of the recompute-selected tokens.
These results support the intended behavior of the CTR mask: it preferentially assigns low-drift, low-attention visual tokens to the cache-reuse path, while preserving recomputation for tokens more likely to affect the action.
They also clarify the scope of the method: LLM cache reuse is a temporally guided approximation for VLA control rather than a theorem of exact equivalence under arbitrary causal-token changes.
